# Harlow hospital gets coroner warning over resuscitation decisions

**A coroner's report says staff at the Princess Alexandra Hospital do not fully understand the rules on do not resuscitate decisions. The hospital has replied, setting out the changes it is making.**

Published by The Seax (https://theseax.com/), independent Essex news. Place: Harlow, Essex, England. Published: Monday 23 November 2026, 07:53 (UK time). Updated: Monday 23 November 2026, 09:09. Section: Courts. Topic: Court.

## Key points

- Trust accepts the form was not signed off by a consultant within 48 hours
- Digital system for recording resuscitation decisions now in use
- The inquest will resume once further evidence on resuscitation is in

## The story

An Essex coroner has written to the Princess Alexandra Hospital in Harlow with concerns about decisions on resuscitation and mental capacity. Assistant coroner Stephen Simblet issued a prevention of future deaths report after the death of Luciana Gianfrancesco, 52, at the hospital. It went to the hospital trust and to Essex Partnership University NHS Foundation Trust (EPUT), which provided her mental health care. The report is dated 2 July 2026 and was published by the Courts and Tribunals Judiciary on 12 November.

The report says she died after being admitted with pneumonia and other health problems. It says doctors decided in April 2024 that she had capacity to refuse treatment. Her family and supported living staff disagreed. The report says this led to some delays in life-saving treatment, which she later agreed to have. It also says a do not resuscitate form was partly filled in by one doctor. Neither her family nor the consultant in charge knew about it, and no senior doctor signed it off.

The coroner raised three concerns. Staff are unsure how to assess capacity when a patient does not always accept life-saving treatment, and how to get advice from psychiatric staff. Clinical staff do not fully understand or follow the steps for making, recording and sharing do not resuscitate decisions. And it is not clear whether the hospital's resuscitation policies meet national standards for patients who are clinically obese. The inquest has not finished. It was adjourned after most of the evidence was heard, while more evidence on resuscitation is gathered.

In its formal reply to the coroner, dated 18 August and published alongside the report, the trust accepts there were shortcomings in how the do not resuscitate decision was documented and recorded. Its own policy says such a form must be endorsed by the patient's consultant within 48 hours, and that did not happen. The trust's cardiac arrest review panel found the records did not show the reasoning behind the decision or how the benefits and burdens of resuscitation had been weighed, and that the decision was not formally reviewed when one of the reasons for it, a high potassium level, improved.

The letter, signed by the trust's chief executive, chief medical officer and interim chief nursing officer, says a digital system for recording resuscitation and treatment escalation decisions, known as ReSPECT, is now in use across the hospital. Staff have been told that obesity or any other single condition must never on its own justify a do not resuscitate decision, and that a mental health diagnosis does not by itself mean a patient lacks capacity. The trust plans more training on resuscitation decisions and the Mental Capacity Act, clearer guidance on when to ask for specialist mental health advice, and continued audits. The letter gives no dates for these steps and has no separate answer on the coroner's concern about resuscitation policies for clinically obese patients.
